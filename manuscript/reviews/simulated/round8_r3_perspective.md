### Summary Assessment
The paper measures what happens to a "predict a dynamical property from one observation" benchmark when the observation happens to determine the generator: a 35-year-old tabulation inverter recovers the CA rule table from a single 127×127 diagram (100% ECA, 97.5% radius-two), re-running the twin-run experiment on the recovered table is *exchangeable* with an independent replicate of the target, and the two learned estimators sit 3–35 replicate-noise units away — with their best-looking target, damage survival, turning out to be mode assignment rather than regression. Round 8 was a presentation-only pass, and on my dimension it largely worked: the abstract now glosses its own jargon, the introduction attaches an explicit consequence to each of the three results, body paragraphs went from median 147 to 128 words (max 465 → 279, p90 264 → 209), the longest caption from 220 to 159 words, and the new Fig. 1 finally shows an ML reader what a twin run, a light cone and an "extent" actually are. ## Weaknesses

### W1: The abstract's opening premise reads as the negation of the abstract's own result — precisely for the SBI audience the revision targets
**Problem**: The abstract opens "Whether and how far a one-cell difference between two runs of a cellular automaton (CA) spreads is defined by *twin* simulations: these damage-response statistics **are not a function of any single observed diagram**." Four sentences later: "a single diagram identifies the rule table for 100% (elementary) and 97.5% (radius-two) of held-out rules", and the closing sentence is "When the observation encodes the generator, system identification … is the baseline to beat." To a simulation-based-inference reader, "not a function of the observation" is heard as *not identifiable from x*; the paper then shows that θ (the rule) *is* recoverable from x, and hence the target is (up to Monte-Carlo noise) a deterministic function of x after all. That is not a contradiction in substance — the statistics are *defined* by an experiment the diagram does not contain, and *recoverable* from it only via the generator — but the abstract never states the resolution, and the resolution is the paper's entire thesis. Related, in the same paragraph: "reconstruct-and-simulate sits at **its reference value**" leaves the reference value unnamed (it is √2), so the sentence's punchline is unavailable to anyone who stops at the abstract; and "the 88 elementary-CA **orbits**" is the one piece of unglossed field jargon left in an otherwise well-glossed abstract.
**Why it matters**: The abstract is where this audience is won or lost, and the tension sits in sentence 1. A skeptical ML reader who resolves it as "the authors are overstating the premise to make the result sound surprising" will stop reading — which would be exactly the wrong conclusion, since the premise is registered and correct under its intended meaning.
**Suggestion**: Keep the registered phrase (it is the pre-registered wording and I am not asking for a claim change), and add the resolving clause immediately after it — e.g. "… are not a function of any single observed diagram: no summary of one run exhibits them. What this paper measures is that one run nevertheless *determines* them, because it determines the rule." Then name the reference: "sits at the √2 value an independent repeat of the target's own measurement attains, while two direct learned estimators are 3–35 times the noise floor." And "the 88 symmetry-distinct elementary rules (orbits)".
**Severity**: Major

### W2: The paper's flagship number for my audience — R² = 0.98 with ρ = 20 — does not match its own Table II, and nothing reconciles them
**Problem**: Sec. IV A states "the CNN's ECA spreading rate reads $0.979$, which sounds like a near miss, and $\rho=20$, which is not" (main.tex ≈ l. 494); the Table III caption repeats "ECA spreading rate reads R²=0.98 for the CNN"; and the introduction's first so-what now says "a conventional R² here can read 0.98". Table II (`tab:pertarget`), ECA spreading rate, deep CNN, gives **0.960 ± 0.019** (l. 575). The two are reconcilable — 0.979 is 0.960 + 1 s.d., and the ρ column is presumably computed from one checkpoint's predictions while Table II reports a five-seed mean (the released audit checks `Table II eca cnn rate = 0.960` against the selected-checkpoint five-seed mean and contains no check for 0.979) — but the manuscript never says so, and the seed-0 convention is footnoted only in Table I (`$^{\ast}$seed-0 checkpoint`), not here.
**Why it matters**: This is the sentence the previous referee called "a masterclass in proper baseline calibration", and it is now load-bearing in three places including the introduction. An ML reader's first act on encountering a striking R²/ρ juxtaposition is to look it up in the table. Finding a different number there, with no note, converts the paper's best rhetorical asset into a bookkeeping doubt — and this paper's whole standing rests on arithmetic hygiene.
**Suggestion**: One clause at each of the three sites, e.g. "the CNN's ECA spreading rate reads 0.979 for the seed-0 checkpoint scored in Table III (five-seed mean 0.960 ± 0.019, Table II)". If ρ is instead computed from the five-seed mean prediction, quote 0.960 and drop 0.979. Either way, add a `Check` for the quoted value to `scripts/audit_manuscript_numbers.py` so the two aggregations cannot drift apart again.
**Severity**: Major

### W3: The new neural-boundary paragraph answers "why a convnet" well but never addresses the half of R3 that named attention — and its supporting probe evidence is the weaker of the two probes
**Problem**: The paragraph (Sec. V, "Why a convolutional family marks the neural boundary deserves stating") makes four moves, three of which I endorse: a synchronous CA update *is* a local convolution, so convolution is the natural inductive bias; the shortcut *incentive* is set by the task rather than the backbone; and "Architecture classes beyond this family are untested here." Two problems. (a) The previous referee's R3 asked specifically to "discuss whether attention-based mechanisms might bypass the shortcut learning observed"; the words attention, transformer, sequence model and graph network appear nowhere in the manuscript. The open problems that *are* named ("a larger reader, a differentiable simulator in the loop, joint reader–simulator training, or corruption-trained networks at scale") are training pipelines, not architecture classes. (b) The clause "the probes show the rule information is accessible there — so representational capacity is unlikely to be what binds" does not distinguish the two probes of Sec. IV D, which answer different questions: the *identity* probe (0.87/0.95 balanced accuracy) shares rule classes between train and test, so it certifies accessibility only for rules the network was trained on; the *truth-table-bit* probe, the out-of-rule transfer measure, reaches only 0.79 (ECA) and **0.61** (radius two) against chance 0.5. For held-out rules — the regime the benchmark scores — the accessibility evidence is weak, and the paper's own dedicated learned rule-reader (Sec. IV F(ii)) recovers 0.73 of table bits, "never a full table exactly", with the MAP variant failing everywhere (R² ≤ −2.2). Separately, "the constrained network and the 354× larger unconstrained one **bracket that family from both ends**" is stronger than the evidence: both endpoints are convnets trained with the same objective, the same 60-epoch budget and no architecture search, and the ResNet18 control is a single seed ("each a single seed on the identical grid cells", Sec. IV F(iii)). That samples a capacity axis; it does not bracket a family.
**Why it matters**: The "your neural baseline was weak" objection is the *first* thing an ML reviewer will raise, and this paragraph exists to absorb it. As written it will half-succeed: the convolution-is-the-right-prior argument is compelling, but a reader who wanted to hear about attention will note that the manuscript declines to say the word, and a reader who checks Sec. IV D will notice that the strongest-sounding probe number is the one that does not transfer across rules.
**Suggestion**: Three sentence-level edits, no new experiments and no claim change. (i) Replace "bracket that family from both ends at this budget" with "sample that family at two capacities differing by 354×, one seed each, under one fixed training budget". (ii) Attribute the accessibility claim to the transfer probe and state its weakness: "the out-of-rule table-bit probe reaches 0.79/0.61 against chance 0.5 — accessible, but far from the inverter's exactness". (iii) Add one sentence naming the untested classes and what would settle it: "Architectures with a different relational inductive bias — attention over rows or cells, graph networks on the neighbourhood lattice — are untested here; the diagnostic that would decide the question for any of them is the one we report for this family (retrieval in the bottleneck versus the regression head, Sec. IV C), not a higher R²." That last clause converts a defensive paragraph into a portable protocol, which is worth more to my audience than either answer.
**Severity**: Minor (in the sense of "text-level and non-blocking"), but it is the most substantive fix on my list after W1–W2.

### W4: The most instructive annotation in the paper's most pedagogical figure is the least legible thing in the manuscript
**Problem**: In Fig. 1, the "extent" bracket on the rule-110 damage panel — the one visual that defines the spreading rate and cone fill — is drawn *inside* the panel at row `TWIN_STEPS-5`, in 6 pt white type with a thin black stroke, on top of the densest yellow-on-black damage speckle (`make_figures.py`, `if rule == 110:`). At the figure's print scale (figsize 7.1 in reproduced at `\textwidth`, so ≈1:1) I could not read the word "extent" at 100% zoom, and at 4× magnification it is still fighting the texture behind it. The bracket also sits ~4 rows above the final row whose extent it measures.
**Why it matters**: Everything else in Fig. 1 is legible, so this is the one place a first-time reader will bounce — and it is the annotation the caption explicitly directs them to ("The bracket on the rule-110 panel marks the final-row *extent*").
**Suggestion**: Move bracket and label *below* the panel frame (`clip_on=False`, y just under the last row, immediately above the statistics line), draw them in the same crimson as the flip marker so the two annotations read as one visual language, and set the label to 7 pt. Anchor the bracket on the final row itself.
**Severity**: Minor

### W5: The figure added to fix the density complaint arrives with the manuscript's longest caption
**Problem**: The caption pass genuinely shortened the old mini-essays (longest caption 220 → 159 words), but the new leader is Fig. 1 at **159 words** — and its last two sentences duplicate the body verbatim in substance: "the targets average n_pairs = 256 such pairs per rule" (Sec. ## Detailed Comments

### Title & Abstract
- The title is accurate and, unusually, states the negative result in the main clause. "Simulation-limited" is good: it tells an ML reader immediately that the winning baseline's error is set by compute, not by learning. It is long, but every phrase is load-bearing.
- Abstract structure is now: premise → protocol → identification result → exchangeability → calibrated comparison → mode assignment → frontier → scope → thesis. That is the right order for my field. Register is much improved: "amortization" is glossed inline ("train a model once, then predict per observation at almost no cost") and "exchangeable" is glossed ("statistically indistinguishable from"). See W1 for the opening tension, the unnamed reference value, and "orbits".
- The Chaos lead quotation ("Machine learning is increasingly used to read dynamical properties … skipping the perturbation experiments that define those properties") is the single most accessible paragraph in the manuscript and will do more recruiting than the abstract. Consider whether one clause of it — "skipping the perturbation experiments that define those properties" — belongs in the abstract too.

### Introduction
- Motivation for an ML reader is now explicit and correctly sourced: amortization framed via `cranmer2020`, the weak-baseline prevalence via `mcgreivy2024`, shortcut learning via `geirhos2020shortcut`. The "genotype vs phenotype" distinction, which is field jargon, is defined on first use.
- The budget-indexed-family paragraph is the conceptual crux for SBI readers (identification is not a point estimator, it is a family indexed by simulation budget, and 1−ICC is the entire headroom a direct estimator can win). It survived the trim with its `vispoel2026structure` parallel and its "with simulation budget now part of the cost" clause restored. Good.
- Paragraph breaking is real and measurable: 49 → 57 body paragraphs, median 147 → 128 words, max 465 → 279, p90 264 → 209, at essentially unchanged body length (7602 → 7474 words). The introduction is where this is most felt.

### Methodology / Research Design
- Not my primary dimension; I defer to R1. From an SBI standpoint the reliability apparatus is exemplary and rarely seen in ML venues: three explicitly distinguished ceilings (ICC(1); independent-replicate agreement 2·ICC−1; large-simulation reference), measured rather than assumed because survival and fill are heteroscedastic, all intervals rule-bootstrapped. The ρ statistic (Eq. 2) is the right normalization and its two reference values (√2 for a matched-budget resimulation, 1 for a perfect latent-mean predictor) are stated as expectation-level with a delta-method account of the sampling variability. I would like to see ρ used more widely in my own field; consider whether it deserves a one-line "portable definition" callout.
- One presentational hazard: Sec. III labels the estimators (i)–(iii) and Sec. IV F labels the frontier analyses (i)–(iv), and cross-references of the form "Sec. IV F(iii)" recur. "(iii)" therefore means "direct convolutional amortizer" in one section and "fairness controls" in another. Naming them at the reference site ("Sec. IV F, *Fairness controls*") would remove the collision.

### Results / Findings
- Completeness is not in question. What is missing, for my audience, is a *picture of a result*: after Round 8 the manuscript has three figures of the phenomenon (Figs. 1, 5, 6) and two of the frontier (Figs. 2, 3), but every headline finding — the calibrated gap, the mode-assignment discovery, the retrieval-matches-the-head diagnostic — lives only in Tables I–III and prose. See "one presentation change" below.
- Table III (ρ) is the paper's best table and is correctly bolded on the mechanistic column, with the "3 of 18 / 1 of 160 noiseless rules drop out of both sums" caveat stated. Table II's negative CI on the five-statistic column (e.g. survival [−0.537, 0.924]) is reported as computed — appreciated.
- The stacking/retrieval subsection (Sec. IV C) is, for me, the most transferable part of the results, and it is the part most likely to be skimmed because it arrives fourth. The nearest-training-rule lookup reaching 0.864/0.883 in the CNN's own bottleneck versus the network's own 0.880/0.847 is the sentence I would put on a slide. Consider promoting the retrieval baseline to its own labelled paragraph heading ("Retrieval baseline: is the score recognition?") so a skimmer cannot miss it.
- Figure/table quality: see W4, W5 and Minor Issues. Figs. 2 and 3 remain dense multi-panel line plots whose curves must be read against ceilings stated in Appendix B rather than on the axes; a horizontal dashed "reduced-budget ceiling" line drawn *in* each panel would remove one lookup per curve.

### Discussion
- The conditional claim is stated well and is the right level of generality: "*a nominally single-observation amortization benchmark is solved by system identification* whenever the observation determines the generator within a known or searchable model class, the inversion is tractable, and the target's own measurement procedure can be re-run at matched budget." That is a testable statement about benchmark design, not a slogan about deep learning.
- The self-limiting paragraph — "We do not name a published benchmark this invalidates: we do not know of one … and we have not surveyed for it" — is the most credibility-earning sentence in the paper for a skeptical ML reader. Do not let a future round soften it.
- The two portable diagnostics ("neither diagnostic is CA-specific, and both are cheap to run on any benchmark whose generator space is densely sampled") are named once, in passing, in that same paragraph. This is the paper's most actionable export and it is currently a subordinate clause; see below.
- The stochastic-CA outlook (Domany–Kinzel as the testbed, the entrywise pseudo-posterior already being the Beta–Bernoulli posterior over π_k) is the correct next experiment and is properly fenced ("We have not run it, so nothing here licenses conclusions for that case").
- Limitations are unusually well handled: the missing marginalizing decoder is called "the uncomfortable gap" in a paper about constructing baselines carefully — an admission most authors would bury.

### Conclusion
- No over-inference detected. Every transfer claim is labelled conjectural ("Each *collapse* is measured on CA; every *transfer* is conjectured, as is the repair ordering"), and Table VI's caption forbids extrapolation beyond the tested corruption model, architecture and training distribution.

### References
- Coverage is appropriate for my dimension: the SBI/ML-for-science anchors (`cranmer2020`, `mcgreivy2024`, `geirhos2020shortcut`, `brunton2016`, `schmidtlipson2009`, `mordvintsev2020`, `gilpin2019`) are present and used, not decorative. Appendix E's Table VII, which tabulates prior identification work by observation regime so the novelty boundary is auditable, is a model of how to do this. Reference-quality judgements otherwise belong to R2.

## Questions for Authors

1. **Is the 0.979 in Sec. IV A and the 0.98 in Table III's caption and the introduction the seed-0 checkpoint, while Table II's 0.960 ± 0.019 is the five-seed mean?** If so, please say so at each site; if not, please explain what differs, because as printed the paper's headline calibration example disagrees with its own per-target table (W2).
2. **On what evidence do you expect an attention-based or graph-based estimator to behave like the convolutional family here?** Your task-structural argument (the shortcut incentive comes from the observation encoding the generator, and a training-set lookup already matches the network) suggests the answer is "any architecture, because the incentive is upstream of the backbone" — but the manuscript never states that inference, and never names the architecture classes the previous referee asked about. Saying it explicitly, with the retrieval diagnostic as the proposed test, would strengthen rather than weaken your scoping (W3).
3. **Would you consider stating the two portable diagnostics as a numbered, named recipe** — (a) stratify within the intermediate band of your target and check whether skill survives; (b) run nearest-neighbour retrieval in your own model's embedding and compare to the model's head — rather than as a subordinate clause in the Discussion? These are the parts of the paper my field can act on tomorrow, and they are currently the least findable.
4. **For the out-of-rule table-bit probe at radius two (0.61 against chance 0.5): do you regard that as evidence that rule information is accessible in the bottleneck for *unseen* rules,** or only that it is accessible for trained rules (identity probe, 0.95)? The Discussion's "capacity is unlikely to be what binds" clause rests on the answer.
