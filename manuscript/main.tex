\title{Simple single-diagram statistics rival deep networks for estimating the
dynamical invariants of cellular automata: a pre-registered benchmark}

\author{Michiel Rollier}
\affiliation{TODO: affiliation}
\author{TODO: co-authors}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The behaviour of a cellular automaton (CA) is most defensibly characterised not
by its update rule but by \emph{dynamical invariants}---quantities such as
damage spreading that are defined through perturbation experiments (evolve two
copies of a configuration that differ in one cell and watch the difference
grow). Such invariants require running the simulator twice, so estimating them
from a single observed space--time diagram is a natural task for a learned
amortiser, and a natural place to ask whether deep representation learning adds
value over hand-crafted features. We answer this with a pre-registered
benchmark. Across the elementary rule space and an enlarged two-state,
radius-two space of $2^{32}$ rules, we compare an anti-shortcut convolutional
amortiser against a regressor on five cheap, label-free single-diagram
statistics, under an explicit observation protocol and leave-rules-out
evaluation. The deep network provides \emph{no advantage}: the simple baseline
matches or exceeds it at predicting the invariants globally, at placing held-out
\emph{complex} (glider-supporting) rules once such rules are made plentiful, and
at resolving behaviour \emph{spatially} in non-uniform CA. We explain why---the
local rule is readable from the diagram and the invariants are strongly
texture-correlated---and turn the negative into two constructive results: a
training-free, interpretable method for spatially-resolved phenotype maps of
non-uniform CA, and a data-driven map of where complex behaviour lives in rule
space (${\approx}\,7.8\%$ of radius-two rules, versus ${\approx}\,2\%$ of
elementary rules). Pre-registration, code, and data are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cellular automata (CA) are the canonical minimal model of emergent
computation: a single local update rule, iterated in parallel, produces the full
zoo of behaviours from frozen order to chaos to the localized, interacting
``particles'' of complex, glider-supporting rules~\cite{wolfram2002,langton1990}.
Classifying that behaviour is a long-standing problem, and a subtle one, because
the field distinguishes the \emph{genotype}---the local update rule---from the
\emph{phenotype}---the mesoscopic structure of the space--time diagram it
generates. A classifier that reads behaviour off the diagram must be prevented
from instead reading the \emph{rule} off the diagram, which is almost always
possible: a diagram contains, in its local neighbourhoods, the entire rule table,
and a convolutional network recovers the elementary rule with $99.9\%$ accuracy
unless architecturally stopped~\cite{rollier2024cnn}. This is a textbook instance
of \emph{shortcut learning}---deep vision models latch onto the most accessible
predictive cue rather than the intended one~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}.

Compounding the difficulty, ``the behaviour class of a rule'' is not a single
well-defined quantity. Formalized Wolfram-style class membership is
undecidable~\cite{culikyu1988}, and the class observed in practice depends on the
initial-condition measure~\cite{gilman1987}, the lattice, and the observation
horizon. The defensible object is therefore not the rule but a \emph{protocol
tuple}---(rule orbit, IC measure, lattice, horizon)---characterized by
\emph{dynamical invariants}. The best-posed of these is \emph{damage spreading}:
evolve two copies of a configuration that differ in a single cell and measure how
the difference grows~\cite{vispoel2024damage}. Damage-spreading scalars stratify
behaviour as theory predicts (dying damage for order, ballistic space-filling for
chaos, sub-ballistic sparse cones for gliders) and, crucially, are defined
through \emph{two} runs of the simulator---so they are not a function of any
single observed diagram.

That last property makes them a natural target for \emph{amortization}: train an
encoder to predict, from one diagram, a quantity that ordinarily requires a
perturbation experiment. If it succeeds and generalizes to unseen rules, one is
tempted to conclude the encoder has learned to read behaviour. But the shortcut
worry returns in a quantitative form: the same textures that betray the rule also
correlate with the invariants, so a deep amortiser might be winning only what a
handful of cheap statistics already capture. Whether deep representation learning
\emph{adds value} over hand-crafted features for this task is thus an empirical
question---and exactly the kind of question that invites, after the fact,
optimistic framing. We therefore \emph{pre-registered} it.

This paper reports the benchmark. Under a fixed observation protocol, across the
elementary rule space and an enlarged two-state radius-two space of $2^{32}$
rules, we compare an anti-shortcut convolutional amortiser against a regressor on
five cheap, label-free single-diagram statistics, with leave-rules-out
evaluation and thresholds fixed in advance. The deep network provides \emph{no
advantage}: the simple baseline matches or exceeds it at predicting the
invariants globally, at placing held-out complex rules once such rules are made
plentiful, and---the one task a scalar baseline seemingly cannot do---at
resolving behaviour \emph{spatially} in non-uniform CA, where a patch-wise
hand-crafted map ties or beats the learned map. We explain why the negative is
structural rather than a tuning artefact, and turn it into two constructive
contributions: a training-free, interpretable method for spatially-resolved
phenotype maps, and a data-driven map of where complex behaviour lives in rule
space. All criteria, code, and data are released.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Rule spaces and the observation protocol}
We use two rule spaces. \emph{Elementary CA} (ECA): two states, radius one, so a
three-cell neighbourhood, $2^{2^3}=256$ rules, of which $88$ are distinct
representatives under the reflection and complementation symmetries that a
behavioural classifier should respect. \emph{Radius-two CA}: two states, a
five-cell neighbourhood, a $32$-entry table and $2^{32}\approx4\times10^{9}$
rules; ECA is the special case whose table ignores the two outer neighbours, so
an embedded ECA reproduces its elementary diagrams bit-for-bit (a continuity
control). Because the space is astronomically large we \emph{sample} it uniformly
and deduplicate by the order-four reflect/complement orbit.

Every quantity below is indexed by a fixed \emph{observation protocol}: a
Bernoulli$(1/2)$ initial condition on a ring of $127$ cells with periodic
boundaries, evolved from $t=0$. We avoid power-of-two widths, under which
additive rules collapse to homogeneity on a periodic ring and diverge from the
infinite-lattice reference behaviour. The horizon is capped so that the light
cone of a single perturbed cell cannot wrap the ring: $\lfloor
w/(2r)\rfloor-1$ steps for width $w$ and radius $r$, which for ECA is the
familiar $\lfloor w/2\rfloor-1$.

\subsection{Damage-spreading invariants}
The targets are four label-free scalars per rule, estimated from twin runs: draw
an initial condition, flip one cell, evolve both copies to the horizon, and
compare. \emph{Damage survival} is the probability that any difference remains;
conditional on survival, \emph{damage fraction} is the fraction of the ring that
differs, \emph{spreading rate} is the cone extent normalised by $2r$ times the
number of steps (so $1$ is light speed for any radius), and \emph{cone fill} is
the density of differing cells within the cone (high for chaos, low for sparse
gliders). Each vector is a Monte-Carlo average over $n_{\mathrm{pairs}}=256$
initial conditions; the residual estimation noise is $\approx0.006$--$0.014$ per
feature, which upper-bounds any amortiser's achievable $R^2$. Each rule's random
stream is keyed on its identity, so a rule's target is a well-defined function of
$(\text{rule},\text{protocol})$, independent of which other rules share the
computed panel (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\subsection{The deep amortiser and the simple baseline}
The \emph{amortiser} is a convolutional network designed against the rule-reading
shortcut: $2\times2$ first-layer kernels (too small to span a neighbourhood and
its output together, unlike a $3\times2$ window that would read a rule-table
entry directly) feeding a signed $64$-dimensional bottleneck and a linear
regression head. A \texttt{predict\_map} variant applies the same head before
global pooling to emit a per-patch map. We use BatchNorm rather than GroupNorm in
the regression encoder: GroupNorm rescales every patch by whole-image statistics,
which leaks global context into the map and destroys its locality (a rule-0
region's predicted survival jumps from $0.01$ to $0.4$--$0.8$ depending on its
partner), whereas BatchNorm's inference-time running statistics keep the map
local (the same probe stays flat to the third decimal). Diagrams are augmented
only by the exact orbit symmetries (cyclic shift, reflection, complementation),
which map a diagram to a genuine orbit partner. Training is $60$ epochs at $127$
px with an anti-shortcut-preserving recipe; five seeds vary the weight
initialization and minibatch/augmentation order while the data split and targets
are held fixed.

The \emph{baseline} is a regressor on five cheap, label-free single-diagram
statistics: the polarity-folded live-cell density, the temporal activity
(fraction of cells that change between rows), the zlib compression ratio, the
$2\times2$ block entropy, and a radius-aware input-entropy variance---Wuensche's
automated glider screen~\cite{wuensche1999}, the temporal variance of the
neighbourhood-pattern entropy. All five are (exactly or approximately) invariant
under the orbit symmetries, so the baseline is denied no symmetry the encoder
enjoys. We report both a ridge and a gradient-boosted-tree fit and take the
stronger as the baseline of record. Crucially, both amortiser and baseline are
trained on the same rules and evaluated with the \emph{same} leave-rules-out
split against the \emph{same} targets. The comparison is deliberately asymmetric
in the network's favour: the amortiser has orders of magnitude more parameters
and capacity than a five-feature regressor, so a failure to \emph{exceed} the
baseline is not a small-model artefact---and any larger network would still have
to beat five scalar statistics.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
Every criterion, threshold, rule panel and control was committed to version
control \emph{before} the corresponding measurement, and no threshold was edited
after a number was seen; the revision history makes the ordering auditable. The
amortiser counts as ``meaningfully better'' only if its held-out median $R^2$
exceeds the best baseline by $\geq0.10$ \emph{and} it wins on $\geq3$ of the four
features---a bar fixed in advance. The complex-rule hold-out set, the map
resolution metric and its verdict rule, and the target-noise bound were likewise
fixed before measurement. This discipline is what lets us report a negative
result without the suspicion that the comparison was arranged to produce it.

\subsection{The complexity landscape and complex-rule placement}
\label{sec:complex}
Under uniform sampling, complex (glider-supporting) rules are
${\approx}\,7.8\%$ of the radius-two space (Wilson 95\% CI $[6.9,8.8]\%$;
$n=3000$), versus ${\approx}\,2\%$ of the 88 ECA orbits---so the intermediate
regime, a two-member club among ECAs, is genuinely populated. Held out entirely,
these complex rules are placed \emph{without systematic bias}---but by the
\emph{baseline} as well as the amortiser (Table~\ref{tab:global}, last row):
populating the regime, not the network, is what removes the bias
(Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}). The complex label here only selects a held-out
subpopulation; the reported quantity is the $R^2$ and signed spreading-rate bias
of the four continuous invariants for those rules, not a classification score, so
the placement test is not circular. The signature thresholds were fixed a priori
by calibrating on the famous complex ECAs (54, 110, 106) embedded into radius
two, which the signature recovers exactly on the 88 elementary orbits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The benchmark returns the same answer three ways: a deep amortiser does not beat
cheap single-diagram statistics at estimating CA damage-spreading invariants---not
globally, not for held-out complex rules in a space where such rules are
plentiful, and not as spatially-resolved maps. We read this as a
\emph{structural} property of the task, not a failure of tuning. Two facts make
it structural. First, the local rule is essentially readable from the
diagram~\cite{rollier2024cnn}, so any behaviour that is a smooth function of the
rule is in principle recoverable by whatever most cheaply exposes it. Second, and
more to the point, the damage invariants are strongly \emph{texture}-correlated:
a chaotic rule looks busy and spreads damage fast; a frozen rule looks static and
does not. Five hand-crafted statistics capture that correlation, and the deep
encoder---trained on the same information---captures no more of it. The
much-touted ``repair'' of complex-rule placement in the enlarged space is
likewise not the network's doing: once the intermediate regime is populated,
\emph{any} competent regressor places held-out complex rules without bias, so the
credit belongs to the rule space, not the learner.

This has a bearing on the natural next idea---replacing invariant regression with
a predictive, forecasting objective. We expect it to inherit the same shortcut:
forecasting future rows is trivial once the rule is recovered, so a ``read the
rule, roll it forward'' baseline would again be hard to beat. The texture- and
rule-readability of ECA-like diagrams appears to be a fundamental obstacle to
deep learning \emph{adding} value here, rather than an incidental one.

The negative has a constructive shadow. Because the invariants are cheaply and
locally predictable, one can build \emph{training-free, interpretable} phenotype
maps for non-uniform CA by sliding the hand-crafted regressor over the diagram;
these resolve behaviour to $\approx16$ cells---as finely as the best learned map,
and without a GPU, training set, or opaque encoder. And the same invariants,
computed over a uniform sample, yield a data-driven picture of \emph{where}
complex behaviour lives (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}): a distinct band at
sub-ballistic spreading and low cone fill, populated at $\approx7.8\%$ in the
radius-two space against $\approx2\%$ among elementary rules.

Our result sits squarely in the shortcut-learning
literature~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut} and in the older recognition that no
unsupervised taxonomy is ``just there'' in data without a chosen
similarity~\cite{kleinberg2002}: here the cheapest similarity---texture---already
suffices, and the deep model adds nothing on top of it. The scope of the claim is
deliberately narrow: it concerns damage-spreading invariants under one
observation protocol on two-state CA. Invariants that depend on genuinely
non-local or rare structure that summary statistics miss---long transients,
specific particle interactions, computational depth---might still reward a learned
representation, and identifying such a target is the natural next step. For the
task as usually posed, however, the honest recommendation is to compute the cheap
features and skip the network.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

All experiments run on a single Apple-silicon machine (Metal/CPU, fp32); the
pipeline is first-party except for the gradient-boosted-tree and ridge baselines
(\texttt{scikit-learn}) and PyTorch. The amortiser error bars come from five
training seeds that vary only the weight initialization and minibatch/augmentation
order; the leave-rules-out split, the diagram cache and the targets are keyed on
their own fixed seeds, so the spread measures optimisation-seed variance alone.

\emph{Target well-definedness.} Each rule's damage-spreading target is estimated
from an initial-condition stream seeded on the rule's identity, so the value is
independent of which other rules are computed alongside it. An earlier
implementation seeded the stream by the rule's position in the sorted panel; we
measured the resulting cross-panel drift at up to $0.05$--$0.08$ on
borderline-survival rules (unbiased Monte-Carlo resampling, but panel-dependent)
and removed it, verifying zero drift under identity keying. The intrinsic
estimation noise at $n_{\mathrm{pairs}}=256$ ($\approx0.006$--$0.014$ per feature)
remains and is reported as the achievable-$R^2$ ceiling.
