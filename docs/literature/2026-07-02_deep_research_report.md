# Is Behaviour-Based Classification of Cellular Automata Well-Posed

## Bottom line

Your empirical result is much more consistent with the foundations literature than it is with the optimistic intuition behind “let SSL discover the behavioral taxonomy.” The strongest published case **against** your original goal is that there is no single, rule-intrinsic notion of CA “behavior” that is simultaneously empirical, rigorous, observation-independent, and algorithmically decidable. Different traditions classify different objects: finite-support evolutions, topological dynamical systems, typical orbits under a measure, attractors, limit sets, coarse-grained emulations, or hand-picked spacetime diagrams. These do not coincide in general, and several of the most natural classification problems are undecidable. On top of that, unsupervised clustering has no bias-free notion of the “right” partition, while modern visual SSL is known to lock onto whichever cues are easiest to stabilize under its objective, often low-level texture-like cues rather than the mesoscopic structures a human would call “phenotype.” citeturn13view0turn17view2turn16view0turn16view1turn36search0turn38search1turn38search0turn39search1turn39search11

The strongest published case **for** a salvageable program is not “discover a uniquely correct unlabeled taxonomy of ECA diagrams,” but something narrower and better-posed: classify **a specified observation protocol**—for example, a rule together with an initial-condition measure, lattice/boundary convention, and observation horizon—or classify rules by **dynamical invariants** such as equicontinuity/sensitivity, damage spreading, particle/domain structure, or coarse-grainability. Those objects are not identical, but they are at least explicit. In other words: the literature supports reformulating the target before investing further, not abandoning the entire enterprise. citeturn9view0turn12search0turn12search3turn21search2turn24search0turn24search4turn24academia30

## Per-rule behavioural classification is not a single well-defined object

**Established.** Cúlik and Yu’s formalization of Wolfram-style classes already shows the core problem: once you try to make the classes precise, membership becomes undecidable. In their scheme, Class One means every finite configuration reaches a stable homogeneous state; Class Two means every finite configuration is eventually periodic; Class Three asks orbit-reachability decidability for finite configurations; Class Four is the unrestricted remainder. They proved it is undecidable whether a CA is in Class One, in Class Two, or in Class Three. Sutner sharpened this by locating these classes in the arithmetical hierarchy: CLASS ONE and CLASS TWO are Π\(^0_2\)-complete, CLASS THREE is Σ\(^0_3\)-complete, and CLASS FOUR is trivial as the complement class. Kari’s later survey summarizes the same point succinctly: even for one-dimensional CA, there is no algorithm that determines which Cúlik–Yu class a given CA belongs to. citeturn13view0turn17view2turn17view3turn16view1

**Established.** The more rigorous dynamical-systems alternatives do not define the same target as Wolfram/Li–Packard. Gilman’s 1987 measure-theoretic scheme explicitly classifies a CA **together with a product measure**: Class \( \mathcal{A} \) is equicontinuous on a set of measure 1, while the other classes are stochastic analogues of equicontinuity and expansiveness; Gilman says outright that Class \( \mathcal{A} \) is measure-independent but the other classes can vary when the measure changes. Kůrka’s topological classification instead partitions one-dimensional CA into equicontinuous, almost equicontinuous, sensitive-but-not-positively-expansive, and positively expansive classes. That classification is exhaustive for one-dimensional CA, but Durand, Formenti, and Varouchas showed that deciding membership in Kůrka classes K1–K3 is undecidable in general; Kari’s survey records the same result and notes that decidability of K4 remains open. citeturn9view0turn12search12turn46view0turn46view2turn16view0turn16view1

**Established.** These frameworks already imply that “the class of a rule” is not uniquely a property of the local update table alone. Gilman’s setup makes the dependence on the initial-condition measure explicit. Hurley’s attractor work makes typicality explicit in a different way: with respect to Bernoulli product measures, a CA either has a minimal quasi-attractor whose basin has full measure, or no chain component has a basin of positive measure; in a companion paper he studies minimal attractors defined measure-theoretically and shows there is at most one minimal attractor for a Bernoulli measure, when it exists. Blanchard and Tisseur then extend the measure-sensitive language around equicontinuity, and later work on \( \mu \)-equicontinuity treats “typical perturbations” rather than all perturbations. So depending on whether you adopt a topological, measure-theoretic, attractor-based, or finite-support notion of behavior, you are classifying different mathematical objects. citeturn21search2turn21search3turn12search0turn12search3turn12search14

**Established.** The observed class also depends on lattice size, boundary conditions, and time horizon. Gilman noted that most computer simulations replace infinite configurations by periodic finite ones and explicitly remarked that this changes the sample space, even if the display often “looks” similar for large enough periods. The Stoop–Martínez line on ECAs gives concrete examples for Kůrka classes under standard infinite-lattice definitions, while many empirical class labels in the Wolfram tradition come from finite random arrays with periodic boundaries and finite observation windows. Even your hoped-for phenotype is therefore not a property of the rule in isolation unless you freeze the observation protocol first. That conclusion is directly stated in Gilman’s setup and is implicit in essentially all later rigorous work. citeturn9view0turn46view0turn46view2

**Contested.** Agreement exists only in limited regions. Rules that are strongly ordered or strongly chaotic often line up across multiple schemes, but borderline rules do not. In the ECA context, Stoop and Martínez use rule 108, 73, 110, and 90 as prototypical examples of K1–K4, respectively, which qualitatively resembles the simple/periodic/complex/chaotic progression. But the very existence of papers showing that rule 40—usually treated as class I—contains chaotic subsystems, and that rule 42—usually treated as class II—has a chaotic global attractor, demonstrates that one scheme’s “boring” rule can be another scheme’s chaotic object once the observational target changes from a generic random display to a specific invariant subset or attractor. citeturn46view0turn28search1turn28search4turn28search3turn28search6

**Unknown.** There is still no broadly accepted theorem-level dictionary translating Wolfram/Li–Packard classes into Kůrka classes, Gilman classes, Hurley attractor types, or limit-set properties for all ECAs. Reviews emphasize the resulting split between theoretical classifications based on topological dynamics and computational theory, and experimental classifications based on simulated spacetime statistics. That split is not a gap in exposition; it is the field’s actual state. citeturn32search0turn31search2

## Class IV and edge of chaos are real in some senses and unstable in others

**Established.** Langton’s original “edge of chaos” claim was never simply “complex rules are rules with a certain λ.” His 1990 paper argued that computation is enhanced near phase transitions between ordered and chaotic regimes and used λ as a control parameter to study ensemble-level trends in rule space. That is a statement about **average behavior across rule ensembles parametrized by λ**, not a theorem that λ uniquely localizes complex computation for individual rules. Mitchell, Hraber, and Crutchfield’s re-examination is the key corrective source: they repeated the Packard-style evolutionary setup and found very different outcomes, concluding that the original interpretation was not correct. They also showed why the density-classification task itself biases evolution toward λ near 1/2, largely due to symmetry and combinatorics, and pointed out that the hand-designed GKL rule with strong computational performance sits at λ = 1/2, right in the middle of the nominally chaotic region rather than at a distinguished “critical” λ\(_c\). citeturn22search0turn43view1turn44view1turn44view2

**Established.** The operationalizations of “complex/class IV” that have had the best qualitative staying power are **particle/domain descriptions** from computational mechanics, **damage-spreading or Lyapunov-style perturbation measures**, and **some finite-size basin/transient diagnostics**. Crutchfield and Hanson’s computational-mechanics program identifies regular domains, defects/particles, and particle interactions in spacetime diagrams; their detailed analysis of rule 54 is a canonical rule-specific demonstration of class-IV-like organization. Wuensche’s input-entropy variance was designed for the same middle ground: ordered dynamics gives low entropy/low variance, fully chaotic dynamics high entropy/low variance, while complex glider-bearing dynamics tends to give higher temporal variance, and Wuensche explicitly presents it as a way to screen out glider rules. Damage-spreading and Lyapunov constructions also have a substantial literature: Shereshevsky defined left/right Lyapunov exponents for one-dimensional CA; Bagnoli, Rechtman, and Ruffo introduced Boolean-derivative-based maximal Lyapunov exponents for ECAs; Finelli, Manzini, and Margara related Lyapunov exponents to expansivity and sensitivity; and more recent work by Vispoel, Daly, and Baetens revisits damage spreading and the Lyapunov spectrum in a unified language. citeturn24search0turn24search4turn26search4turn43view2turn25search8turn25search4turn25search5turn12search16turn31search16

**Contested.** None of these operationalizations is a universally valid definition of “class IV.” Wuensche’s own paper is careful: the method “seems” to correspond to subjective judgment, and he notes a serious finite-size caveat—when colliding and non-colliding zones coexist in large systems, input-entropy variance can shrink and goes to zero in the infinite-size limit in the coexistence picture. Zenil’s compression-based program similarly reports clusters corresponding to Wolfram’s classes and proposes phase-transition coefficients related to sensitivity and universality, but those claims remain heuristic rather than theorem-level, and the literature has not converged on algorithmic-complexity surrogates as definitive class-IV detectors. The same is true for λ: it is useful descriptively at the ensemble level, but the Mitchell–Hraber–Crutchfield critique remains the standard reason not to treat it as a per-rule complexity coordinate. citeturn43view2turn27search0turn27search2turn43view1turn44view1

**Established.** Several results directly demonstrate fragility of any claim that “Wolfram class = dynamical fact.” Ohi showed that rule 40, usually labeled class I and also placed in Braga’s C\(_1\), nevertheless has chaotic properties on suitable invariant sets. Chen, Hsu, and others showed that rule 42, normally regarded as Wolfram class II, has a chaotic global attractor with positive topological entropy and is chaotic in the senses of Li–Yorke and Devaney. Terry-Jack and coauthors’ 0–1 test for chaos on ECAs reports that almost all Wolfram-chaotic rules are confirmed as chaotic, but two are not. Those are exactly the kinds of results that say “class IV” and its neighbors are not stable under change of lens. citeturn28search1turn28search4turn28search3turn28search6turn28search5turn18search11

**Established.** In the ECA universe, standard Wolfram class IV is indeed tiny. Many expositions treat 54 and 110 as the canonical class-IV ECAs, while other operational schemes sometimes elevate 106 as a nearby outlier or “edge” rule. Reviews comparing many ECA classifications emphasize precisely this instability: the same rules recur as special, but the exact membership of a “complex” category varies with the formalism. Wuensche’s work, however, also makes the opposite point at larger rule sizes: glider-supporting complex rules become plentiful enough that automated screening yields an “unlimited source” for study, and he explicitly studies the distribution of such rules as neighborhood size grows. So the literature supports the statement that class-IV-like behavior is **sparse in ECA but not obviously sparse in richer rule spaces**—with the caveat that this is a family resemblance, not a single invariant category. citeturn29search4turn32search0turn30search16turn43view2

**Unknown.** There is still no agreed per-diagram scalar or per-rule invariant that everybody accepts as “the” operationalization of class IV. The field has many proxies and exemplars; it does not have a canonical definition. citeturn32search0turn31search2

## Genotype, phenotype, and the identifiability trap

**Established.** The literature now explicitly separates **rule-table classifications** from **spacetime-pattern classifications**. Vispoel, Daly, and Baetens frame this distinction directly in their 2022 review: some classifications are based on the CA’s rule table, others on the generated spacetime pattern, and much of the literature stays confined to ECAs. That already matches your genotype/phenotype language. citeturn31search0turn32search0

**Established.** There is now direct prior art for your exact concern that image models “cheat” by recovering the local rule. Rollier, Daly, and Baetens show that earlier CNN approaches to automated ECA classification had effectively been trained to identify the local update rule rather than mesoscopic behavior; in their own experiments, a default CNN reached 99.84% validation accuracy at inferring the local rule from the spacetime diagram. Their proposed remedy is architectural trimming plus augmentations designed to erase microscopic cues while preserving mesoscopic structures. Those interventions enlarge the gap between rule identification and behavior-class identification, but they do not prove that rule information can be made absent—only less easily available. citeturn45view0turn45view3

**Established.** There is also a principled reason that “suppress rule identifiability” is hard. Local structure theory, Wuensche’s input-entropy statistics, and the ergodic/invariant-measure literature all rely on the fact that different rules induce different mesoscopic block-frequency trajectories, attractor basins, or invariant measures under random starts. Gilman’s paper is explicit that with random product-measure inputs one should study the CA together with the chosen measure; Gutowitz’s hierarchical scheme classifies rules by their action on \(n\)-step Markov measures; Wuensche’s input-entropy method uses rule-table lookup frequencies generated by spacetime evolution. Put bluntly: the same published tools you want to use to identify behavior are themselves largely extracting **rule-specific statistical fingerprints**. An expressive image model discovering those fingerprints is therefore not anomalous. That last sentence is an inference from the cited literature, but it is a strong one. citeturn9view0turn35search19turn34search1turn43view2

**Contested.** I do **not** find a theorem proving that any sufficiently expressive model must recover the exact rule from finite spacetime windows whenever the rule is recoverable in principle. The literature is suggestive, not definitive, on that stronger claim. There are partial reasons to expect identifiability from finite windows in many practical ECA settings: some diagrams contain all neighborhoods needed to reconstruct the rule table; Rollier et al. explicitly decompose diagrams into neighborhood contributions and show how the local rule can be encoded from channel maxima; and for additive or ergodic families there are strong results about invariant measures and entropy constraints. But “behavior-level equivalence classes of diagrams cannot be defined without smuggling in the rule” is too strong as a theorem-level statement on the basis of the current record. citeturn45view0

**Established.** Israeli and Goldenfeld offer the most promising rigorous alternative if you want a phenotype-like hierarchy that is not just a relabeling of local rules. Their coarse-graining construction produces local coarse-grained descriptions for rules from all Wolfram classes, including some universal and therefore undecidable CA. They show that renormalization-group flows induce a hierarchy of CA rules and explicitly propose that hierarchy as a candidate complexity measure and classification method. Later work extends the renormalization idea to probabilistic CA and related settings, but the 2006 Israeli–Goldenfeld paper remains the central source. This line is attractive for your project because it defines equivalence through **emulation at larger scales**, not through pixel-level resemblance. citeturn35search0turn35search1turn35search2turn35search3

**Unknown.** There is very little evidence that current SSL objectives can be constrained to eliminate genotype information while preserving only a human-desired phenotype, except by injecting hand-chosen invariances and mutilating the data in a targeted way. That is not the same as discovering a label-free taxonomy; it is a very strong prior in disguise. citeturn45view3turn39search1

## Unsupervised discovery is impossible without bias and SSL will use the easiest bias available

**Established.** Kleinberg’s impossibility theorem is directly relevant here. No clustering function can satisfy the simultaneously natural desiderata of scale invariance, richness, and consistency. So a procedure that “lets the data decide the natural behavioral groups” cannot avoid making extra choices somewhere—metric, representation, augmentations, density model, number of clusters, or inductive bias. The ugly-duckling tradition makes the same point more philosophically: without privileging some predicates over others, there is no non-arbitrary notion of similarity. In your setting, that means no unsupervised CA taxonomy is “just there” in the data. A taxonomy appears only after you decide what counts as similarity between diagrams. For the ugly-duckling claim I am relying on widely used secondary expositions of Watanabe rather than a directly queried primary source. citeturn36search0turn36search2turn37search10turn36search1

**Established.** Modern image models are known to prefer shortcut cues. Geirhos and coauthors showed that ImageNet-trained CNNs are strongly texture-biased relative to humans, and their later perspective paper generalized this as “shortcut learning”: deep models tend to rely on the most predictive accessible signal, not the one users intend. That is already a near-perfect description of your failed BYOL run. If a rule leaves a stable texture fingerprint across random starts, image SSL has every incentive to use it. citeturn38search0turn38search1

**Established.** SSL adds another layer of objective-induced bias. Xiao et al. argue that contrastive learning hard-codes augmentation-defined invariances and can fail when downstream tasks require information the augmentation suppresses. Wen and Li show theoretically that contrastive learning can preferentially learn dense nuisance-like features while suppressing sparser semantically aligned ones, depending on the data model. Fine-grained SSL work also reports that standard contrastive pipelines often learn low-level texture information and poor localization, with RandomResizedCrop singled out as part of the problem. Ericsson, Gouk, and Hospedales add a broader benchmarking result: no single SSL method dominates universally, and feature retention differs across representations. Together these papers imply that if you want a dynamical-phenotype representation, **you must shape the objective around dynamical invariants**; vanilla instance discrimination is not neutral. citeturn39search1turn39search11turn39search13turn38search3turn38search7

**Established.** Deep-learning work directly on CAs has reproduced the rule-vs-behavior tension rather than resolving it. Your own cited chapter with Daly and Baetens explicitly frames the need to divert the network away from microscopic local updates and toward mesoscopic patterns, and shows that tailored architecture plus augmentation can help in a supervised Li–Packard task. But that is still a **supervised** success, with hand-designed biases and known labels. I do not find convincing published evidence that fully unsupervised visual SSL has already solved the stronger problem you care about: discovering a robust phenotype taxonomy while provably resisting rule identification. Across adjacent dynamical-systems ML, the more successful representation-learning approaches are predictive or operator-based—world models, latent-dynamics models, Koopman-style representations—not static image embeddings. That suggests a likely path forward for CA too, but it remains an extrapolation rather than an established CA-specific result. citeturn45view0turn40search9turn40search10turn40search19

**Unknown.** I do not see a published impossibility theorem stating that unsupervised learning cannot discover a useful CA taxonomy in principle. The impossibility is weaker and more practical: there is no unique unsupervised taxonomy without added priors, and current image-SSL priors are badly aligned with your intended semantics. citeturn36search0turn38search1turn39search1

## Non-uniform and heterogeneous cellular automata are not just ECAs with interpolation noise

**Established.** The non-uniform CA literature treats heterogeneity as a genuine enlargement of the dynamical object, not a small perturbation of the uniform case. Dennunzio, Formenti, and Provillard’s foundational papers begin by comparing the dynamical behavior of non-uniform CA to classical CA and emphasize that several similarities and differences appear immediately; they establish decidability results for basic properties such as surjectivity and injectivity and develop equicontinuity notions suited to the non-uniform setting. Their later work on local rule distributions and language complexity treats the **distribution of local rules itself** as a structural object affecting dynamics. citeturn41search5turn42academia11turn42search2turn42search4turn42search7

**Established.** Recent reviews continue to stress that most classification schemes are still ECA-centric. Vispoel, Daly, and Baetens explicitly say that many currently available classifications are limited to ECAs. The broader taxonomy by Rollier and colleagues also argues that non-uniform CA deserve their own systematic treatment as a CA family rather than an afterthought. So there is, at present, **no strong evidence base** that an ECA-derived behavioral taxonomy transfers cleanly to heterogeneous rules. citeturn32search0turn41search6turn31search15turn41search1

**Contested.** There are hints that heterogeneity creates genuinely new behavior classes or at least new prevalences. Hybrid/non-uniform constructions can produce rich glider and collision dynamics that are more plentiful than in rule 110, and the non-uniform literature repeatedly notes that letting rules vary in space increases expressive power. But these are mostly existence and construction results, not a settled classification theory. They support your intuition that heterogeneous CA may exhibit localized interfaces and composite phases, but they do not yet justify assuming that an ECA taxonomy will organize them. citeturn30search4turn41search4

**Unknown.** I do not find good evidence that ECA behavioral labels transfer to nuCA in any principled sense. If anything, the literature suggests the opposite default: once local rule distributions become part of the model, they should probably become part of the classification target as well. citeturn42search2turn41search5

## Verdict and priority reading

**The strongest published case that your original goal is ill-posed.** If the goal is “from unlabeled spacetime images alone, discover the true behavioral taxonomy of ECA rules,” the literature is stacked against it. The formal reason is that CA “behavior” has several inequivalent rigorous meanings, many classification problems are undecidable, and some classifications depend explicitly on the initial-condition measure or the attractor notion rather than on the rule alone. The methodological reason is that clustering has no bias-free truth criterion, while CNN/SSL pipelines preferentially exploit the easiest stable cues—in CA images, those are often rule-specific statistical textures. Your BYOL failure is therefore not just an engineering accident; it is exactly what one should expect if the representation objective is not explicitly aligned with a chosen dynamical notion of phenotype. citeturn13view0turn17view2turn9view0turn21search2turn36search0turn38search1turn45view0

**The strongest published case that the project is salvageable.** It is salvageable if you reformulate the object of classification. The best-supported reformulations are: classify **rule–measure–observation-protocol tuples** rather than bare rules; classify by **dynamical invariants** such as equicontinuity/sensitivity, damage spreading, Lyapunov exponents, particle/domain structure, or basin/transient statistics; or classify by **coarse-graining/emulation relations** in the Israeli–Goldenfeld sense. For machine learning specifically, the literature points away from static image SSL and toward objectives that force the encoder to preserve dynamical information—predictive latent dynamics, perturbation-response matching, operator learning, or metric learning on explicitly computed invariants. If your four-scalar damage-spreading baseline already isolates \{54, 106, 110\}, that is not a disappointing baseline; it is evidence that the right target may be an invariant-based taxonomy rather than an appearance-based one. The latter point is an inference from your result plus the cited literature. citeturn12search16turn31search16turn24search0turn24search4turn35search1turn40search9turn40search10

**What looks simply unknown.** No paper I found establishes a canonical, broadly accepted, per-diagram definition of class IV; no paper proves a clean theorem that expressive image models must recover the rule whenever they can; and no body of evidence shows that an ECA taxonomy transfers cleanly to non-uniform CA. Those are genuine open ends, not missing citations. citeturn32search0turn31search15
